On How We See Time

Jim Robin Schlupp  ·  2026

For as long as I can remember, the year has been a shape.

The very bottom is New Year — where December folds into January.

It turns counter-clockwise — up the right, over the top, down the left.

It's July — past half the year. But my dot hasn't reached the top.

The top isn't mid-year — it's summer break. Five weeks that, to a kid in Zürich, were the year's other pole. A calendar built at ten, still running.

So when I picture something ahead, I'm hovering just above now — looking down the road in front of me.

Two months off, and I'm zoomed right in. Six, and the ring pulls back until I can see the whole bend.

Almost no one sees the year the way you just saw mine.

Ask around, and it splits into a hundred shapes — a line, a loop, a staircase, the paths of a childhood garden. Commonest first.

The line

The plainest answer — the year as a straight run, left to right, no bend at all. When people draw on a blank page, this is the one that wins.

Eagleman battery; Laeng & Hofseth, 2019

The loop

A closed ring, usually January at the top, running clockwise. The other of the two most common answers — and the family mine belongs to. So I'm less alone than the line made it seem; I just run it backwards, and start at the bottom.

Laeng & Hofseth, 2019 · 75% run it clockwise

The corridor

Numbers down a hallway that recedes to a vanishing point — the year running away into depth, not around a circle. Rarer, and fully three-dimensional.

Gould et al., participant “AB,” 2014

Then it gets stranger.

The rarer forms — about one in ten of us, and no two quite alike.

So how rare is any of this, really?

The honest answer is a moving target — it depends entirely on how strictly you ask.

1 in 23

carry some form of synesthesia — about 4.4%. The first study to sample people at random, not volunteers, found it far more common than the old guess of 1 in 2,000.

Simner et al., 2006 · N = 1,690

Most think they see the year. Almost none do — reliably.

People like me — only this, nothing else

The average synesthete has about 3.5 kinds at once. But time‑space sits apart: a statistical island that barely predicts having any other form. One of the few that stands alone.

Novich/Eagleman 2011; Ward & Simner 2022

It runs in families

Around 40% of synesthetes have a close relative with it — yet identical twins can differ, and the hunt turns up five chromosomes, not one gene. No single switch.

Asher 2009; Tomson 2011; twin studies

the old myth · 6 : 1
the real ratio · ≈ 1 : 1

And it isn't a women's trait

Early studies said six times more common in women. Sample at random instead of by who volunteers, and the gap vanishes — the sexes are essentially equal.

Baron-Cohen 1996 → Simner et al., 2006
